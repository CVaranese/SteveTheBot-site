SteveTheBot Upgrade Map
SteveTheBot · updated 13 Nov 2026

A Captain Falcon that learns Melee, on the way to Project+

A reinforcement-learning bot from 2017, revived and retrained on a fast Melee simulator. This page has three parts: the roadmap (what each part of the system is now and what comes next), progress (the newest results first), and how the trainer works. Model names are explained on the rankings page.

Strongest in the simulatorT22LongBase7PPO, self-play, 72 min on the GPU, 5,880 updates (double the usual length). Wins 98 of 100 against the frozen T21L6Cs4, the best model before it. T22LongBase8 also wins 98. Not yet played in Dolphin.
How it plays (watched)Better than random. Well-timed aerials, but no follow-ups or chasing after a hit. Recovers well. That was a 2,940-update model; the double-length ones have not been watched.
Now and nextIn progress: learning from human Melee Falcon replays (T-4). Imitation alone plays badly (0 of 50 against the level 9 CPU), but PPO started from it did better than PPO from scratch: one run won 21 of 50 against the level 9 CPU, the best model with the human-like reaction delay so far (from scratch: 1 to 3 of 50). In Dolphin against the level 9 CPU, the best score is a hand-edited model (50 of 50, no training); the best trained-only models win 49 of 50. Tested and not adopted: memory of past frames (T-37, below plain inputs). Also open: scoring in Project+ (T-27).

Where learning starts

testing
NowRandom starting weights. Everything the bot knows came from playing itself. This is the "zero line".
In progress · T-4
  • Learn from human Melee Falcon replays first (imitation), then self-play from there. Human play is the likeliest source of combos and follow-ups, which self-play has not found. The replays and the zero line are kept as two lines, each the other's control.
  • Data: HAL's public dataset, 1,264 Falcon-versus-Falcon games on Final Destination, used privately. The simulator cannot replay them exactly, so a second input layout ("input 2") was built from fields both have.
  • Tested: a first imitation model copies the inputs it was already holding and wins 0 games, even against the level 1 bot. About half its deaths are self-destructs: it walks off the stage with no up-B and the double jump unused. Counting frames where the human changes input five times more raised accuracy on those frames from 0.045 to 0.239, but did not fix recovery.
  • Guess, not measured: imitation alone meets states the replays seldom show. This is the usual weakness of behaviour cloning (copying a human): once the bot drifts off the human's path, it has never seen where it landed, and errors pile up.
  • Tested 13 Nov, all 1,236 games (20.6 million samples): held-out cross-entropy (lower is better) 0.620 plain against 0.612 with 4 past snapshots 8 frames apart; accuracy 0.840 against 0.841; on frames where the input changes 0.083 against 0.087. History helps only slightly in this 2x128 network.
  • Built and running, 13 Nov: PPO self-play that starts from the imitation weights (--init-from), with an optional KL penalty (--kl-to, --kl-coef): an extra cost for drifting away from the frozen imitation policy, so PPO cannot quickly throw away what it copied. The imitation policy starts with entropy 0.68 against about 3.99 for a fresh network, so PPO from it explores little.
  • Tested 13 Nov, results (2,940 updates each, wins of 100 against four frozen from-scratch models; 2 runs per setting, and they differ a lot):
    • The imitation model alone, though it gets 84% of held-out inputs right, wins about 1 of 100 against the from-scratch models and 0 of 50 against the level 9 CPU in Dolphin. Copying inputs is not the same as playing well.
    • PPO from the imitation weights: mean 62 (72 and 52), against 48 for PPO from scratch on the same opponents. One run won 21 of 50 against the level 9 CPU, the other was not played in Dolphin. The gain is likely but not firm with two runs. Entropy rose from 0.68 to about 2.3, so PPO made the policy much more random; how much human play survives is not measured.
    • KL penalty: made it weaker. A light hold (0.1) won a mean 38 and 12 of 50 against the level 9 CPU (the other run 33). A strong hold (1.0) stayed as weak as the imitation model (mean 1.5). The policy it is held to is weak, so holding close to it holds PPO weak. Whether it makes the bot look more human is not checked (not watched).
    • Frame history (the last 4 states) on the imitation start did not help after PPO either: mean 45 and 49 wins, about the from-scratch level.
Decided 11 to 12 Nov: start from replays, in two lines.

The controller

timing changedheads not default
Now · changed 12 NovOne of 54 fixed actions: 9 stick positions times one button or none. New PPO models (sim/ppo_run.py) choose an action every frame, and each action reaches the controller 11 frames (0.19 s) later, like a human reaction time (your choice, 0.19 s x 60 frames, rounded). Models trained before this, and the old 2017 learner, choose every 2 frames with no delay.
Cost: at the usual 2,940 updates a delayed model is far weaker than an undelayed one, because the opponents it is scored against have no delay. Tested with a delay of 15: 10 of 50 wins against the level 9 CPU in Dolphin, against 42 to 46 without. With the default delay of 11, T17F54 (below) won 3 of 50. The double-length models (T22Long) were trained without the delay.
How timing works · tested
  • Each saved model has a small settings file: frames per action and delay. Missing means every 2 frames, no delay.
  • An action chosen on frame t reaches the controller on frame t+11. Meanwhile the network also sees the 11 actions still in flight: 796 + 11 x 54 = 1,390 inputs.
  • Discount and GAE decay are scaled to the shorter step, so one update covers the same game time.
  • Self-play delays both sides. The scripted opponent is not delayed.
  • The same button on consecutive decisions is one continuous hold, in Dolphin and the simulator (A held gives one jab). The bot cannot press a button twice without another action between.
Resumed models keep their own settings; --frames-per-action 2 --delay 0 gives the old behaviour.
Tested 12 Nov: two heads (T-17)A second action set of 102 pairs: 6 buttons x 17 stick positions (centre, 8 full, 8 half-tilted). A two-head network picks a button, then a stick position given that button. With delay 11, 2,940 updates, two seeds each, the 54 actions beat both two heads (74 wins of 100 head to head) and one flat list of the 102 pairs (72). Two heads vs the flat 102 list was even (48). Against the level 9 CPU in Dolphin the 102-pair models won 1 to 3 of 50.
Reading (a guess, not tested): at this training length the larger action set is not learned yet. The 54 actions stay the default. No decision yet on what to do next with the two heads; a C-stick head was planned after them. The replay labels show what the 54 cannot say: the C-stick is used on 9.4% of human frames, and several buttons at once on 3.2%.

One score per skill: the scenario yardstick (T-28, T-31)

Whole-game wins mix every skill together and swing a lot between runs. The simulator can save a whole match and load it again exactly. So moments are saved from earlier games (an opponent knocked off the stage; the frame a first hit lands). Every model is then put into the same saved positions and plays on. The result is one score per skill. The positions come from our own bots' games, not human games.

Edgeguard and recovery are judged by outcome: the offstage player gets back on the stage and can act, or loses the stock. Starts that are already lost or already safe are removed first, by replaying each one with the best model recovering and nobody guarding. A start stays only if the player has to act and needs at least a second. That leaves 170 of 380 starts. (The critic's value guess was tried as the filter and agreed with the replays too weakly, so it is not used.) Three neutral tests were added.

A start loaded from a save has an empty delay queue, so a delayed model stands still for its first 11 frames; on the shield test (attack 8 or 9 frames away) it is always hit, so that score is not fair to delayed models. For input version 2, each player's movement over the last frame and how long its current animation has run are estimated at the start from that one saved frame (95.7% and 81.6% of values exact on random play). The delayed-model rows above are not in the table, which shows undelayed models only. Queued, not built: tech chase and human start positions (T-29). Part 3

How the trainer works

Each block explains one idea in the current trainer (sim/ppo_run.py), with this project's own numbers.

One update, start to finish

  1. Play64 matches run at once. The actor picks an action for each player: every frame at the new default, 256 decisions per match (every 2 frames: 128). Same game time either way.16,384 per side (8,192 at 2 frames)
  2. ScoreEach decision gets its reward, and the critic guesses the value of every state.reward: stocks, damage
  3. JudgeGAE turns rewards and guesses into an advantage: how much better each action went than expected.λ 0.95, discount 0.995 per 2 frames (scaled for every frame)
  4. Learn4 passes over the batch in 4 slices. Actor and critic both take small gradient steps.16 steps, lr 6e-4
play the matches 87%learn 13%

One update takes about half a second for a run alone on the GPU. A usual run is 2,940 updates (about 3,800 to 4,900 games); the double-length option is 5,880. Self-play learns from both players' views, since both are the same network.

Two networks: one acts, one judges

The actor reads the state and gives a probability for each of the 54 actions. The bot samples one, so it does not always do the same thing in the same spot. The critic reads the same state and guesses how much reward is coming. They share no weights.

The advantage, and how GAE estimates it

Learning needs to know whether an action was good. The raw reward is too noisy (a stock is lost seconds after the mistake), so the trainer asks: did things go better or worse than the critic expected? That difference is the advantage A. A positive A makes the action more likely next time, a negative one less likely.

δt = rt + γ·V(st+1) − V(st) · At = δt + (γλ)·δt+1 + (γλ)²·δt+2 + …

δ is a one-step surprise: the reward plus the critic's new guess, minus its old guess. GAE adds up the surprises that follow, each weighted less. λ sets the trade: near 0 trusts the critic (low noise, but biased when the critic is wrong), near 1 trusts the real rewards (unbiased, noisy). The discount γ says how much future reward counts at all.

The PPO clip: learn a lot from each batch, but not too far

The 2017 learner used each batch once and threw it away. PPO goes over the same batch 4 times. That is only safe if the policy cannot drift far from the one that played the games, so PPO compares the new and old probability of each action (the ratio r) and stops rewarding changes past a band of ±ε.

r = πnew(a) / πold(a) · actor loss = −min(r·A, clip(r, 1−ε, 1+ε)·A) − 0.01·entropy

The learning rate, and how to tell it is too small

The learning rate is the size of each weight change. Too large and training swings or collapses; too small and the network barely moves. The usual check is approx_kl: how far one update moved the policy, measured as a divergence between the old and new action probabilities. PPO runs commonly sit near 0.01.

Entropy: how random the bot still is

Entropy measures how spread out the action choice is. Picking uniformly among 54 actions gives 3.99; always picking one action gives 0. The trainer adds a small bonus for entropy (0.01) so the bot keeps trying things instead of settling early. The 2017 learner stayed at 3.93, close to random. The first PPO models ended near 3.3, and the 6e-4 constant runs end at 2.7 to 2.8 (1.7 to 2.5 for the double-length runs). The game report found that much of the remaining randomness is harmless: in 57% to 70% of decisions the character is mid-move and no input could change anything.

Self-play, frozen opponents and seeds

  • Self-play: the same network plays both sides, so its opponent improves with it. The risk is learning to beat only itself, which is why scores also use other opponents.
  • Frozen opponent: a saved model that no longer learns. It is a fixed bar to measure against. When everything beats it, a stronger one is frozen (so the yardstick moved from PPO8Self to T21L6s3 to T21L6Cs4).
  • Seeds: the same training repeated with different luck. Identical settings have ended 10 to 13 wins apart on an easy opponent and up to 30 apart on the hardest, so each setting gets at least two runs, and plain runs in the same batch, before a decision.
  • Rule for a new default: fixed before the runs, so results cannot bend it. Approved 12 Nov: against frozen T21L6Cs4, a change must beat plain runs from the same batch by at least 10 wins on average, with no run below the lowest plain run. The embedding passed it at 2,940 updates and was then set aside once runs were doubled, so close calls need more seeds, not longer runs.
