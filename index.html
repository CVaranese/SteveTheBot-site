SteveTheBot: roadmap
SteveTheBot · updated 16 Nov 2026

A Captain Falcon that learns Melee, on the way to Project+

A reinforcement-learning bot from 2017, revived and retrained on a fast Melee simulator. It learns by playing itself and is scored against fixed opponents and the game's own computer player. The goal is a Falcon that plays Project+ (P+), with tools to see what its network has learned.

Other tabs: Experiments (every test, with results and verdicts), Models (scores per opponent; the "Milestones" filter is one model per change; name explanations; training curves), Watch (every game and scenario page).

Where it stands

  • Newest result
    Eight runs trained 12 times the usual length (578 million decisions, about 8.5 hours each). With the 54 actions, strength keeps rising in a straight line: about 100 Elo per doubling of training, no ceiling yet (chart below).
  • Controller
    The three-head controller (button, stick, C-stick) stayed about 330 Elo behind the 54 actions and climbed at half the slope. Two heads (one run) caught up to the 54 actions at about 200 million decisions and stayed level. The 54 actions stay the default.
  • Against the game's CPU
    Level 9 computer player in Dolphin, 50 games each: the long runs won 11 to 19; the model started from human replays (usual length) won 21. Older models without a reaction delay won up to 49. The in-game CPU is not a strong opponent, so these numbers separate models only loosely.
  • How it plays
    Watched: better than random, well-timed aerials, recovers well, few follow-ups. The model started from human replays "looks much more human" (watched 13 Nov).
  • In progress
    The T-42 batch finished training on 16 Nov and is being scored: the three-head controller started from imitation, more two-head seeds, all at 12 times length. No results yet.
  • Routine yardstick
    Frozen pool 2 (four saved models; a model's score is its share of games won against them), plus the level 9 CPU in Dolphin. A setting's strength is reported as the mean Elo curve of at least 3 seeds; there is no single "peak" number.

The roadmap, stage by stage

The training loop runs from the first stage to the last and repeats. Now means being worked on or tested at the moment; Next is chosen or queued.

0Where learning starts

DoneRandom start (the "zero line"). Imitation from human Melee Falcon replays (a private dataset of 1,264 Falcon-versus-Falcon games) built, with a second input layout that both the simulator and the replays can fill.

ResultImitation alone plays badly (0 of 50 against the level 9 CPU). PPO started from it beat PPO from scratch (mean 62 against 48 wins of 100 on the first pool), and one run won 21 of 50 against the level 9 CPU. Holding PPO close to the imitation policy (a KL penalty) and frame history did not help.

NowMore seeds from the human start, to settle whether the gain is real (scored; the read of the results is pending).

1The game

DoneA native Melee simulator instead of Dolphin: 128 matches at once per run (64 before 13 Nov), up to 6 runs sharing the graphics card. Dolphin stays for watching and for scoring against the real CPU.

NowP+ runs in headless Dolphin at about 12 times real time, and a Melee-trained model has played whole P+ games through a translator (91% of states map exactly).

NextScore models in P+ itself. Decided: no Melee strength bar before P+ work.

2What the bot sees

Done796 numbers for the current frame (both players' position, damage, facing, animation, speeds), plus the actions already chosen but not yet sent (see the controller). Input version 2, built from fields both the simulator and the replays have, is at least as good as the old one.

Set asideAn animation embedding (fell behind plain inputs at double length), input rescaling, the previous action, frame history (below plain inputs at both lengths). So no memory core (recurrent or transformer) for now.

3The network

DoneTwo separate networks, actor and critic, two hidden layers of 128. Four fixes at the PPO build: both hidden layers activated, no sigmoid in the critic, orthogonal starting weights, gradient clipping.

TestedSize: 2x128, 2x256 and 3x256 were not separated at usual length (pool 2 mean 30.5 for 2x128, 34.5 for the best larger one, 3x256 at a lower learning rate). 2x128 stays the default; larger networks need a lower learning rate. Tested again at 12 times length in the T-42 batch plan.

NextOne shared body with a policy head and a value head (its own task).

4The controller

DoneOne of 54 actions (9 stick positions times one button or none). New models choose every frame and each action arrives 11 frames (0.19 s) later, like a human reaction time. At the usual length a delayed model scores far below an undelayed one, because its opponents have no delay.

TestedTwo heads (button, then stick) and three heads (plus a C-stick, 510 combinations): both built. At 12 times length the 54 actions lead (see above); the three-head design is not dropped.

NowThree heads started from human replays, and more two-head seeds (the T-42 batch, being scored).

5Reward and learning step

DoneReward: +1 or −1 per stock, ±0.01 per percent of damage (unchanged). Learning: PPO with GAE, learning rate 6e-4 held constant, 128 matches per update. Doubling run length made every model beat its own shorter copy.

NextOpen: tune play-time randomness before each score, skip decisions where no input matters, mix opponents.

6The opponent

DoneTraining: itself, or a simple scripted Falcon. Scoring: frozen pool 2, the Elo ladder of 13 fixed opponents for long runs, the level 9 CPU in Dolphin, and the scenario yardstick (below).

NextA mix of self-play, frozen past versions and the scripted bot, so it cannot over-fit to one opponent.

7Seeing inside

DoneA log per update, the scored benchmarks, the scenario yardstick, and a game report that plays one game in the animated viewer (Watch).

NextThe report averaged over many games and run on a human player's own games, then tools to ask the network what it would do in a chosen situation.

One score per skill: the scenario yardstick

Whole-game wins mix every skill and swing between runs. The simulator can save a match and load it again exactly, so moments from earlier games (an opponent knocked off the stage, the frame a first hit lands) are saved, and every model plays on from the same positions. Edgeguard and recovery are judged by outcome (back on stage and able to act, or stock lost). Starts that are already lost or already safe are filtered out first.

Against the frozen T21L6Cs4 (170 to 300 starts): recovery 83% to 92% for PPO models against 7% for random play, the one skill that is learned well. Edgeguard 5% to 11% against 6%: the bots barely edgeguard. Follow-up damage 8 to 13 percent against 3.5. Whiff punish 21% to 29% against 16%. All tested 12 Nov; samples can be watched on Watch.

How the trainer works

One block per idea, with this project's own numbers. The sliders and buttons below are live.

One update, start to finish

  1. Play128 matches run at once. The actor picks an action for each player, every frame (256 decisions per match).32,768 decisions per side
  2. ScoreEach decision gets its reward, and the critic guesses the value of every state.reward: stocks, damage
  3. JudgeGAE turns rewards and guesses into an advantage: how much better each action went than expected.λ 0.95, discount 0.995 per 2 frames
  4. Learn4 passes over the batch in 8 slices, so each step uses 8,192 samples. Actor and critic take small gradient steps.32 steps, lr 6e-4
play 87%learn 13%

Playing the matches is most of an update's time (measured at 64 matches). A usual run is 1,470 updates (48 million decisions per side); the double-length option is twice that. A bigger batch means fewer, steadier updates: 64, 128 and 256 matches ended level, and 128 was chosen because more runs then fit in memory.

Two networks: one acts, one judges

The actor reads the state and gives a probability for each of the 54 actions. The bot samples one, so it does not always do the same thing in the same spot. The critic reads the same state and guesses how much reward is coming. They share no weights.

The advantage, and how GAE estimates it

Learning needs to know whether an action was good. The raw reward is too noisy (a stock is lost seconds after the mistake), so the trainer asks: did things go better or worse than the critic expected? That difference is the advantage A. A positive A makes the action more likely next time, a negative one less likely.

δt = rt + γ·V(st+1) − V(st)  ·  At = δt + (γλ)·δt+1 + (γλ)²·δt+2 + …

δ is a one-step surprise: the reward plus the critic's new guess, minus its old guess. GAE (generalised advantage estimation) adds up the surprises that follow, each weighted less. λ sets the trade: near 0 trusts the critic (low noise, but biased when the critic is wrong), near 1 trusts the real rewards (unbiased, noisy). The discount γ says how much future reward counts at all.

The PPO clip: learn a lot from each batch, but not too far

The 2017 learner used each batch once and threw it away. PPO goes over the same batch 4 times. That is only safe if the policy cannot drift far from the one that played the games, so PPO compares the new and old probability of each action (the ratio r) and stops rewarding changes past a band of ±ε (the clip).

r = πnew(a) / πold(a)  ·  actor loss = −min(r·A, clip(r, 1−ε, 1+ε)·A) − 0.01·entropy

Learning rate and entropy

The learning rate is the size of each weight change. A useful check is approx_kl: how far one update moved the policy (PPO runs commonly sit near 0.01; from memory). The old default (3e-4, falling to zero) moved it about ten times less than that, so the second half of a run learned almost nothing. Doubling the total step size (6e-4, held constant) lifted wins against a frozen opponent from 54 to 92 of 100; doubling again (1.2e-3) did not help. So 6e-4 constant is the default.

Entropy measures how spread out the action choice is: picking uniformly among 54 actions gives 3.99, always picking one gives 0. A small entropy bonus (0.01) keeps the bot trying things. The 2017 learner stayed at 3.93, close to random; the 6e-4 runs end at 2.7 to 2.8. The game report found that much of what remains is harmless: in 57% to 70% of decisions the character is mid-move and no input could change anything.

Self-play, frozen opponents and seeds

Self-play: the same network plays both sides, so its opponent improves with it. The risk is learning to beat only itself, so scores also use other opponents.

Frozen opponent: a saved model that no longer learns, a fixed bar to measure against. When everything beats it, the bar is raised. Pool 2 (15 Nov) is T4V2s3, T4HumP1 (started from human replays) and two points of the long ladder run. Plain 54-action runs win 26% against it at the usual length and 63% at 12 times; three heads win 12% and 27%. A pool is never edited: a refresh adds the next number.

Seeds: the same training repeated with different luck. Identical settings end about 10 wins of 100 apart, so differences under about 20 with two seeds are "not separated", and each setting gets at least two runs, with plain runs from the same batch, before a decision.
