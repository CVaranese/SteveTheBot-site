Memory of past frames

13 Nov 2026 · T-37 · Inputs

Abstract

The bot's network has no memory: each decision sees one snapshot of the game, which holds speeds and how far each character is into its current move, but not what happened half a second ago. The simplest way to give a network a memory is to stack the last few snapshots in its input. This experiment asks whether that makes a stronger bot, as a test before building a network with real memory. Six self-play runs were trained, two per input: plain inputs, 4 states 8 frames apart, and 8 states 4 frames apart. All six were scored at the usual length and again after being continued to twice that length, against four frozen models in the simulator and against Melee's level 9 computer player in the Dolphin emulator. At the usual length both history inputs scored below plain inputs against the frozen models: 36% and 41%, against 48%. At double length, 4 states 8 apart nearly caught up (58% against 62% for plain, 50% head to head), while 8 states 4 apart stayed behind (45%; 36% head to head). Against the computer player every model won few games, and the plain runs won the most. Frame history never beat plain inputs, so no network with real memory was built.

Question and hypothesis

The bot's network has no memory: each decision sees one snapshot of the game. The snapshot holds speeds and how far each character is into its current move, but not what happened half a second ago. The simplest way to give a network a memory is to put the last few snapshots side by side in its input ("frame stacking"). This experiment asks whether that makes a stronger bot at the usual training length, and then at twice that length. A network with real memory (a recurrent or transformer core) was to be built only if stacking helped.

Written in the experiment plan on 12 Nov 2026, before the runs:

a variant wins if its mean wins is at least 10 above the same batch's plain runs and none of its runs is below the lowest plain run.

Plain: mean about 50 of 100 against T4V2s3 to s6 (same settings, other seeds).

H4s8: mean 50 to 65; H8s4: 45 to 65 (bigger first layer, slower to learn). Most likely outcome: neither passes the rule at this length (the state already holds speeds, position and action frame).

Expected (guess, written before): plain _c08 75 to 95 vs T4V2; history catches up partly: vs T37Plain _c08 H4s8 mean 35 to 50, H8s4 35 to 50, so history still does not pass (a pass would need clearly above 50 here).

The first quote is the rule a variant had to pass. "H4s8" is 4 states 8 frames apart and "H8s4" 8 states 4 frames apart. "T4V2s3 to s6" are four saved plain models with the same controls, used as fixed opponents; "_c08" is the copy of a run at double length. The last quote was written on 13 Nov 2026, before the runs were continued.

Discussion

At the usual length both history inputs scored below plain inputs. Against the four frozen models plain inputs won 48%, 4 states 8 apart 36% and 8 states 4 apart 41%. The rule of the first quote asked for a clear lead over plain; both are behind it. The third quote had expected them level or ahead.

At double length 4 states 8 apart nearly caught up; 8 states 4 apart did not. Against the frozen models the double-length runs won 62% (plain), 58% and 45%. Head to head against the double-length plain runs, 4 states 8 apart won 50% and 8 states 4 apart 36%.

History never beat plain. At neither length does a history input pass the rule, and at double length the better of the two is level with plain, not ahead.

Against the game's computer player the plain runs won the most games, with few wins for any model. Table 4 gives the games won at level 9; all the models act with a reaction delay there.

The plain runs at double length scored lower than expected. The last quote expected the double-length plain runs to win a clearly larger share against the frozen models than they did; plain won 62%.

Limitations

  • Two runs per input. The differences at double length between plain and 4 states 8 apart are within the spread of two runs.
  • Two history layouts, both covering about half a second. Longer windows and networks with real memory were not tried.
  • One network size. A wider input into the same small network may simply learn more slowly; the experiment does not separate that from the history being of no use.
  • The second half of each run used a different seed from the first half.
  • The scores were measured on the copies saved at 2,940 and at 5,880 updates.
