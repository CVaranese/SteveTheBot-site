Strength on one scale: the ladder

14 Nov 2026 · T-43 · Yardsticks

Abstract

Question. Can a model's strength after long training be predicted from the first part of its run?

Finding. Every saved copy of ten earlier runs played nine fixed opponents, giving each copy one strength number (Elo). Averaged over 4 to 6 runs, strength rises steadily with the logarithm of training and does not flatten within twice the usual length. A single run is too uneven: extending its early line misses its later strength by 85 Elo and mis-ranks the frame-history variants.

Decision. Curves are read as averages over several runs. The long batch (12 times the usual length, the entry above) went ahead on that basis.

Question and hypothesis

Screening runs are short. A setting that is behind at the usual length might be ahead after much longer training, so the project wanted a way to estimate a model's later strength from the early part of its run. Work on board games reports that strength on the Elo scale rises in a straight line against the logarithm of training. This experiment checks, with stored copies of ten earlier runs and no new training, whether that holds here and whether a line through the first half of a run predicts its second half.

Written in the experiment plan on 13 Nov 2026, before the runs:

The straight line in log(decisions) fits worse than the board-game papers report: R2 at least 0.9 in at most half of the 10 runs.

The extended line over-predicts _c08 on average (gains shrink), by 30 to 100 Elo, and its typical miss is not smaller than the "no further gain" baseline for the T40 runs, but smaller for the T37 runs (which gained about +14 wins of 100 in their second half).

Order of the T37 kinds at _c08 (measured before: plain, then 4 states 8 apart, then 8 states 4 apart): the extended lines give the same order. Coin flip; if they do not, the curve cannot rank variants.

One checkpoint's strength is known to about +-30 Elo (450 games); random play is more than 600 Elo below the pool.

"Decisions" measure the amount of training. "_c04" and "_c08" are a run's fourth and eighth saved copy; for the T37 runs the eighth is at twice the usual length, for the T40 runs at the usual length. "R2" is how well a straight line fits (1 is exact). "The pool" is four models trained from scratch that define zero on the scale. "T37" are the runs of the frame-history experiment, "T40" those of the matches-per-run experiment.

Discussion

One run's curve is too uneven to extend. In Table 3 a run's strength often falls from one copy to the next by more than the error of a single value. The straight line fits well in only a few of the ten runs (the R2 columns of Table 4), as the first quote expected.

A line through a run's first half does not predict its second half better than assuming no further gain. The "Extended line" result under Table 4 gives the typical size of both misses; they are about the same. The extended line is too high on average and "no further gain" too low.

The extended lines mis-rank the frame-history variants. For the two plain runs, the two runs with 4 states 8 apart and the two with 8 states 4 apart, the columns "Line through c01-c04, extended to c08" and "Measured c08" of Table 4 put the two history variants in opposite orders. The third quote called this a coin flip and said that in this case the curve cannot rank variants.

Every run gained with each doubling of training. The slope column of Table 4 is positive for all ten runs, and its mean over the T37 runs and over the T40 runs is what the record's table gives as the mean gain per doubling (98 Elo and 95 Elo).

The error of one strength value is smaller than expected, and random play is far below the pool. Both are in the fourth quote; Table 2 and the line under Table 3 give the measured values.

Limitations

  • The abstract says the averaged curve rises steadily and does not flatten. The stored output holds the per-run values (Tables 3 and 4), not a fit of the averaged curves, so that statement is not shown directly here; the site's curve graphs linked in Table 7 draw the averages.
  • A measure of how much a single run moves up and down around its own line was part of the original record; it is not in the stored output, so it was removed from the record and is not given here.
  • Three doublings of training at most. A straight line over that range says little about much longer training, which the next experiment tested.
  • Two of the ladder's partly trained copies are weaker than a copy saved earlier in another run, so the ladder's rungs are not evenly spaced.
  • One strength number per model assumes that strength is transitive; the fit does not test that.

Decision

Curves are read as averages over several runs. The long batch (12 times the usual length, the entry above) went ahead on that basis.
