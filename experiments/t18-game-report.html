Game report

11 Nov 2026 · T-18 · Yardsticks

Abstract

Question. What does one game look like decision by decision, and which inputs could have mattered?

Finding. The bot throws moves at nothing and rarely follows up; when it is free to act, it chooses between about 3 options. The critic tracks the next few seconds, not the stock lead.

Decision. The game report pages on this site come from this; the same report is meant for the user's own games later.

Question and hypothesis

Win counts say whether a model wins, not how it plays. To the eye the first PPO models looked poor: jittery, throwing moves at nothing, rarely following a hit with another. This experiment builds a report of one game that puts numbers on that impression, and in particular counts how many of the model's inputs could have changed anything at all. The simulator can copy a match exactly, so every decision can be replayed with every possible action.

Written in the experiment plan on 11 Nov 2026, before the runs:

Decisions: locked 30%, steer only 20%, free 50%.

When free: about 15 distinct outcomes of 54 on average.

Entropy over the 54 actions: about 3.3 overall (the trained value), a little higher when locked (3.5) than when free (3.0). Effective entropy when free: about 1.8.

Attacks: about 20% hit; more than half of the whiffs start with the opponent more than 30 units away.

Openings: about 1.2 hits and 12% damage per opening; under a quarter of openings get a second hit.

Dash-dances: fewer than 5 in the game.

Critic: its estimate follows the stock lead (correlation above 0.5).

These guesses were written for one game of the model PPO8Bot against a frozen copy of the model PPO8Self. A "whiff" is a move that hits nothing; an "opening" starts with the first hit on an opponent who was in control; "entropy" is the randomness of the choice over the 54 actions (the largest possible value is about 3.99); the "critic" is the network that estimates future reward; the "stock lead" is the difference in lives left.

Setup

Five games were reported, one each: four models against a frozen copy of PPO8Self (PPO8Bot, PPO8Self itself, a hand-edited PPO8Bot with a recovery circuit, and T9Prev, a model that sees its previous action), and PPO8Bot against the scripted bot at level 9. All models choose an action every second frame.

Discussion

Most decisions could not change anything. In every one of the five games more than half of the reported model's decisions were locked (Table 2). The first quote guessed far fewer locked and far more free decisions than the table shows.

Few inputs change what the character does. The row "Inputs that changed what the character did" of Table 2 is close to one decision in ten for every reported model.

When a model is free to act it is choosing among a handful of things. Table 2 gives the number of different things an input could make the character do when free, and the effective number the policy chooses between; both are far below the 54 actions, and below the second quote's guess. The effective number is about three for the unedited models and about two for the hand-edited one, whose choices were sharpened on purpose.

Most moves hit nothing, and most of those were started from far away. The rows "Whiffed" and "Whiffs started more than 40 units from the opponent" of Table 3 show this for all five games; the share of whiffs is lowest in the game against the scripted bot. No game had a dash-dance.

The critic does not track who is ahead. The fifth quote expected its estimate to follow the stock lead. Table 4 shows a negative correlation with the stock lead in these games and a positive one with what happened next (the discounted reward that followed).

Limitations

  • One game per model, one seed. The numbers describe these five games, not the models in general; averages over many games were not made because a report of this kind takes minutes per game.
  • A "follow-up" is any second move landed before the opponent regained control. It is not proof of a true combo.
  • The decision classes depend on the 30-frame window and on replaying the recorded inputs after the tried action.
  • This kind of report works only for models that choose an action every second frame with no reaction delay.
  • The abstract's sentence on following up rests on the openings rows of Table 3, which differ widely between the five games.

Decision

The game report pages on this site come from this; the same report is meant for the user's own games later.
