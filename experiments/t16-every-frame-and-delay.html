Every frame, with reaction delay

12 Nov 2026 · T-16 · Controls

Abstract

Every frame costs nothing; a 15-frame delay costs a lot (10 of 50 against the level 9 CPU, the others 42 to 46).

Every frame without delay plays about as well as every 2 frames. A 15-frame delay costs a lot at this training length: it wins 10 of 50 against the level 9 CPU, the others 42 to 46.

Question and hypothesis

Until this experiment the bot chose an action every second frame and its choice took effect at once. A human player can act on any frame, but reacts to what happens only after about a fifth of a second. This experiment changes both: a decision every frame, and a reaction delay of 15 frames (a quarter of a second) between choosing an action and the controller receiving it. It asks what each change costs at the usual training length.

Written in the experiment plan on 12 Nov 2026, before the runs:

T16F1 vs T16Base: about equal or better per game second of experience (finer control, guess).

T16F1D15: clearly weaker on every yardstick against undelayed opponents (Firoiu et al. 2018: performance falls sharply with delay); vs frozen T21L6Cs4_c04 below 20 wins of 100, level 9 scripted below the base runs.

"T16Base" is the setting used so far (every 2 frames, no delay), "T16F1" decides every frame with no delay, and "T16F1D15" decides every frame with the 15-frame delay. "Frozen T21L6Cs4" is a saved copy of the strongest model so far, used as a fixed opponent; "level 9 scripted" is the project's hand-written bot at its level 9.

Discussion

A decision every frame, without delay, cost little. Against the level 9 computer player the every-frame model won 46 of 50 games and the every-2-frames model 42 of 50 games, one model each. Against the scripted bot the two settings are level (90% and 87%). Against the frozen model the every-frame runs are somewhat lower (9% against 18%), with a wide gap between the two every-2-frames runs ("Each run", Table 4).

The 15-frame delay cost a great deal at this length. The delayed runs won 0% against the frozen model and 2% against the scripted bot, as the second quote expected. Against the level 9 computer player the delayed model won 10 of 50 games.

The comparison is not on equal terms. A delayed model plays opponents that react instantly, and the frozen opponent decides every second frame. The scores say what the control settings cost against these opponents, not which setting learns best against its own kind.

Limitations

  • Two runs per setting, and one model per setting against the computer player.
  • One training length. Whether delayed models catch up with more training was not tested here.
  • One delay length. The delay later made the default is shorter than the one tested here.
  • A run that decides every frame makes twice as many decisions per second of game time, so "equal game time" is not "equal number of decisions" (Table 2).
  • Table 5 holds more games for the every-2-frames setting than for the others: the score log holds a second scoring of one of its models against the scripted bot, and the table pools both.
  • The scores were measured on the copy saved at the end of each run.
