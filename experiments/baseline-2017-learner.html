The 2017 learner as a baseline

11 Nov 2026 · T-1 · Method

Abstract

Barely above random: it never wins a game.

Barely above random: it never wins a game and its choices stay close to random. Models trained only in the simulator play at least as well against the real CPU as the one trained in Dolphin.

Question and hypothesis

The project began in 2017 with an actor-critic learner that trained against the game's own computer player in the Dolphin emulator. In November 2026 that learner was ported to current tools and also moved, unchanged, onto a fast simulator of the game. Before any change of method, this experiment measured how well the original learner plays, so that later methods have a baseline.

No expectation was written down before these runs. They predate the project's experiment plans, and the record was written afterwards from the logs.

Setup

Three trained models and random play are compared. One model was trained in the emulator against the game's computer player. Two were trained in the simulator, one against a copy of itself and one against a hand-written scripted bot. All use the same learner and network.

Discussion

No model won a game. Random play and all three trained models lost every scored game (Table 2), so the games-won column cannot rank them. The columns of stocks taken and lost per game carry the comparison.

The trained models are above random play, by a small margin. Each trained model took more stocks per game than random play did, and each still took fewer than one stock per game while losing all of its own (Table 2).

The yardstick is the training opponent of one of the models. The model trained against the scripted bot took the most stocks from it. That model was scored on the opponent it trained against, so its lead over the other two is not evidence of generally stronger play.

Limitations

  • One run per setting. Differences between the three trained models are not separated from run-to-run variation.
  • The second sentence of the abstract (simulator-trained models against the game's own computer player) rests on a separate first pass in the emulator with a small number of games per model. Those games were logged in a form this page cannot tabulate, so that comparison is not shown here.
  • The abstract says the models' choices stay close to random. That reading comes from the entropy column of the training logs, which this page does not draw.
  • The emulator-trained model was saved partway through its run and has a different number of updates from the simulator models.
  • The scripted bot is a simple hand-written opponent; a later experiment showed it stops separating stronger models.

Decision

The baseline came first, on the user's instruction; PPO was the first change of method.
